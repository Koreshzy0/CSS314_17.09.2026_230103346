### Phase 2 — Strong Scaling vs. Weak Scaling

**Strong Scaling**

The strong scaling experiment used a fixed workload of 1,200,000 elements. Execution time decreased from 1394.977 ms with one process to 335.942 ms with eight processes. The observed speedup was 4.152×, with parallel efficiency of 51.9%.

The scaling was not perfectly monotonic. At five processes, execution time increased to 404.437 ms compared with 391.395 ms at four processes. This may be caused by scheduling overhead and measurement variability.

**Amdahl's Law**

The measured Stage A time was 31.869 ms, giving an estimated serial fraction of 0.022845 (2.28%). The theoretical Amdahl speedup limit was 43.773×, whereas the observed speedup at eight processes was 4.152×. The difference indicates additional parallel execution and process-management overhead.

**Weak Scaling**

At eight processes, execution time was 431.998 ms, weak scaling efficiency was 0.577, and Gustafson's scaled speedup was 7.083×. This demonstrates that the system can process a larger workload in parallel, although efficiency decreases as the number of processes increases.

## Phase 3 — Empirical Roofline and Cache-Blocked GEMM

### Part A: Empirical Roofline

### Part B: GEMM Optimization

Three matrix multiplication implementations were evaluated using 256 × 256 matrices and eight worker processes.

The Naive i-j-k implementation achieved **0.097112 GFLOP/s**. The reordered i-k-j implementation achieved **0.079100 GFLOP/s**, while the best cache-blocked implementation achieved **0.076787 GFLOP/s**.

The tile-size sweep tested block sizes of 8, 16, 32, 64, 96, and 128. Among the cache-blocked configurations, **BS=32** produced the best result, with a tile footprint of 24 KB and operational intensity of 2.667 FLOP/byte.

Interestingly, the Naive implementation outperformed both optimized variants. This suggests that, for this relatively small matrix and pure Python implementation, loop and multiprocessing overhead may outweigh the benefits of improved memory access patterns.

## Phase 4 — Scheduling and Load Balancing

### Scheduling Strategies

Three scheduling strategies were evaluated using 1,600 tasks distributed across eight processes. The workload was intentionally skewed: tasks with larger indexes required more iterations.

The Contiguous Static strategy achieved a median execution time of **113.734 ms**, with an IQR of 8.038 ms. Its worker load imbalance ratio was 5.927, indicating that assigning consecutive task ranges resulted in unequal workloads.

The Cyclic Static strategy achieved the best overall median execution time of **111.694 ms**, with an IQR of 8.907 ms. Its worker load imbalance ratio was 2.342. Distributing tasks in a round-robin pattern helped spread expensive tasks more evenly among workers.

### Dynamic Chunk Scheduling

Dynamic scheduling was tested with chunk sizes of 1, 8, 32, and 64.

The best dynamic result was obtained with chunk size 32, achieving **115.184 ms** and an IQR of 4.102 ms. Chunk size 1 required 118.750 ms, chunk size 8 required 138.784 ms, and chunk size 64 required 116.761 ms.

Dynamic scheduling did not outperform Cyclic Static in this experiment. The shared counter and synchronization operations introduced additional overhead. Small chunks can increase synchronization frequency, while larger chunks can reduce scheduling flexibility.

Some worker active-time measurements were displayed as 0.0 seconds after rounding. Consequently, the very high imbalance ratios for dynamic chunk sizes 8, 32, and 64 should not be treated as reliable quantitative measurements.

### Numerical Verification

All three scheduling approaches produced identical output values. The maximum absolute error was **0.0**, and numerical verification passed.

### Phase 4 Conclusion

Cyclic Static Scheduling achieved the fastest overall execution time in this experiment. It distributed the skewed workload more effectively than Contiguous Static Scheduling without requiring synchronization through a shared task counter.

Dynamic scheduling provided flexibility, but its synchronization overhead prevented it from achieving the best execution time. The results demonstrate that the most effective scheduling strategy depends on workload distribution, chunk size, and coordination overhead.
